About

I study how carbon pricing reshapes the distribution of local air pollution, and whether climate markets can widen the exposure gaps that disadvantaged communities already face.

I am an environmental and resource economist. I received my Ph.D. in Resource Economics from the University of Massachusetts Amherst in 2025.

My research sits at the intersection of environmental economics and environmental justice. CO2 controls such as cap-and-trade also move co-pollutants like SO2, NOx, and fine particulates, which are local and unevenly mixed. Using theory and quasi-experimental methods, I ask who gains and who loses from those shifts, including in regions outside the regulated market that are exposed through electricity trade and pollution transport.

I have taught introductory microeconomics and game theory at Dartmouth College, and development economics and econometrics at UMass Amherst.

ResearchWorking papers

Leaking Environmental Justice: Evidence from the RGGI CO2 Cap-and-Trade Program

Job market paperwith Nathan W. Chan

Regional CO2 markets cap emissions across participating states, but the co-pollutants that accompany fossil generation are local. This paper asks whether the Regional Greenhouse Gas Initiative (RGGI) changed disparities in co-pollutant exposure by race and income, both in regulated states and in unregulated states exposed to leakage. We track emissions from power plants to receptor counties with a reduced-complexity air quality model and estimate effects with a difference-in-differences design that allows heterogeneous treatment effects. We find that the policy's effects on exposure were unevenly distributed, falling short for counties with larger Black populations and lower incomes, in regulated and neighboring unregulated regions alike.

Optimal CO2 Prices and Environmental Justice

Working paperwith John K. Stranlund

We characterize CO2 pricing under environmental justice constraints that prevent existing inequities in ambient co-pollutant concentrations from worsening. In a model of joint control of CO2 and one non-uniformly mixed co-pollutant, uniform CO2 pricing at marginal damage is efficient if and only if the co-pollutant is itself regulated efficiently subject to the constraints. Otherwise, efficient CO2 prices depart from marginal damage and generally vary across firms. The same condition governs when a uniform-price CO2 market is cost-effective.

Regional CO2 Markets and the Clean Energy Transition

Working papersole-authored

Does a subnational carbon market change the structure of the electricity sector? Using state-level panel data from 1999 to 2019 and a difference-in-differences design, I estimate RGGI's effects on generation and installed capacity by fuel. RGGI significantly reduced non-renewable generation and capacity, driven mainly by coal retirements. Renewable generation and capacity rose modestly, but the estimates are not statistically significant. Decarbonization under RGGI came mostly from retiring fossil plants rather than from building new renewables.
